\begin{frame}{Problem 5: From-Scratch Strategy}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item ``From scratch'' means I may use only input, output, arithmetic,
          and the interface toolkit. No other built-in or library function
          (project description).
    \item Arithmetic alone cannot raise a number to a \emph{real} power.
          The identity that makes it possible is $b^{x} = e^{x \ln b}$ for $b > 0$.
    \item So I must write my own $\ln$ and $\exp$. These are the
          \textbf{subordinate functions}:
  \end{itemize}
  \medskip
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    Function & How it works & What it is for \\
    \midrule
    \texttt{absolute}   & sign test                & tolerance checks; sign of $b$ \\
    \texttt{floor\_int} & floor division           & split $x = n + f$, $f \in [0,1)$ \\
    \texttt{pow\_int}   & repeated squaring        & exact $|b|^{n}$ in $O(\log n)$ steps \\
    \texttt{ln}         & series + range reduction & $\ln|b|$ for the fractional part \\
    \texttt{exp}        & Maclaurin series         & $e^{f \ln|b|}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \medskip

  {\footnotesize The only built-in conversion left anywhere in the calculator
  is the \texttt{float()} that reads a text entry, which is an input function.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Problem 5: Four Measured Improvements over D1}
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{1. Range reduction in $\ln$.} Write $b = m \cdot 2^{p}$ with
  $m \in [1,2)$, so $\ln b = \ln m + p \ln 2$ and the series only ever sees a
  small number. D1 rejected bases beyond about $10^{5}$; D2 handles
  $10^{\pm 15}$ in 11 terms or fewer.
  \smallskip

  \textbf{2. Cancellation in $\exp$.} For $y < 0$ the raw series adds and
  subtracts large nearly-equal terms. D2 computes $e^{y} = 1/e^{-y}$ instead.
  \smallskip

  \textbf{3. Rounding down, not toward zero.} \texttt{floor\_int} uses floor
  division, which keeps $f \in [0,1)$ for negative exponents and removes the
  last built-in conversion from the mathematics.
  \smallskip

  \textbf{4. Range-safe order of multiplication.} The answer is
  $a \cdot p_1 \cdot p_2 \cdot q$; the \emph{order} decides whether it can be
  reached.
  \smallskip

  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{lrr}
    \toprule
    Case & D1 & D2 \\
    \midrule
    $\ln$ terms at $b = 10^{15}$              & fails (cap)          & 10 \\
    $\exp$ relative error at $y = -30$        & $6\times10^{7}$      & $3\times10^{-16}$ \\
    $a=10^{-300},\, b=10^{300},\, x=2$        & overflow             & $10^{300}$ \\
    $a=6.7\!\times\!10^{248},b=-6.73,x=-390$  & overflow             & $3.6\times10^{-15}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \smallskip

  \raggedright On the last row, evaluating $b^{x}$ before applying $a$ loses
  $15\%$: that intermediate is subnormal, where a double holds barely two
  digits. The ordering never forms it.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Problem 5: Exception Handling Design}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The project description allows either built-in or custom exception
          classes. I wrote my own so that each kind of failure has its own
          name. All derive from a common \texttt{F5Error}:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \texttt{InputError}: an entry cannot be read as a finite number
      \item \texttt{DomainError}: the input is outside the real domain of $ab^{x}$
      \item \texttt{ConvergenceError}: a series ran past its iteration limit
      \item \texttt{RangeError}: the result is too large or too small to represent
      \item \texttt{AlgorithmRangeError}: the result is representable, but an
            intermediate value of the algorithm is not
    \end{itemize}
    \texttt{F5Error} is never raised directly, so one \texttt{except} clause
    catches every calculator error.
    \item \textbf{Why this matters.} In D1, hitting the iteration limit raised
          \texttt{OverflowError}. Running out of iterations and producing a
          number that is too big are different failures with different fixes,
          and are now separate. Every message states the cause \emph{and} a
          corrective action (NFR-03).
    \item \textbf{Two defects this design exposed, both fixed.} D1 returned
          \texttt{inf} for an out-of-range result, so the window showed
          ``f(x) = inf'': no cause, no action, against NFR-02. And a zero
          multiplier short-circuited the domain rules, so $a = 0$, $b = -2$,
          $x = 0.5$ returned $0$ instead of being rejected under FR-04.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Appendix: Subordinate Function Details, \texttt{ln}}
  For $m \in [1,2)$ and $t = (m-1)/(m+1)$:
  \[ \ln b = 2 \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^{2k+1}}{2k+1} + p \ln 2,
     \qquad b = m \cdot 2^{p} \]
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Because $m$ is between 1 and 2, $|t| \le 1/3$, which is what makes
          the series converge quickly for every base.
    \item $\ln 2$ is computed once by the same series. Nothing is taken from a
          table of constants.
    \item The iteration cap raises \texttt{ConvergenceError}, so the loop can
          never run forever.
    \item Measured relative error is at most about $2 \times 10^{-12}$ against
          the reference implementation, across $b \in [10^{-15}, 10^{15}]$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Appendix: Subordinate Function Details, \texttt{exp} and \texttt{pow\_int}}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{exp}: Maclaurin series, each term built from the previous
          one. Negative arguments go through $e^{y} = 1/e^{-y}$ to avoid
          catastrophic cancellation. At $y = -30$ the D1 error was a factor of
          $6\times10^{7}$; D2 is about $3\times10^{-16}$.
    \item \texttt{pow\_int}: exponentiation by squaring, meaning the exponent
          is halved at each step, so $O(\log n)$ multiplications instead of
          $n$. Whole-number results come out exact.
    \item Worked examples and a flowchart for each of the five functions are in
          \texttt{docs/flowcharts/} in the repository.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Appendix: Range-Safe Order of Multiplication}
  \footnotesize
  The answer is the product of four factors: $a$, the two halves
  $p_1, p_2$ of the integer power $|b|^{n}$, and the fractional factor
  $q = e^{f \ln |b|}$. Multiplication is associative in mathematics but not in
  floating point.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $a = 10^{-300}$, $b = 10^{300}$, $x = 2$ has the ordinary answer
          $10^{300}$, yet $b^{2} = 10^{600}$ overflows first, and multiplying
          by $a$ afterwards cannot recover it.
    \item Reaching the subnormal range and coming back destroys most of the
          significant digits. On $a = 6.7 \times 10^{248}$, $b = -6.73$,
          $x = -390$ the answer is an ordinary $7.85 \times 10^{-75}$, but
          $b^{x}$ alone is $1.17 \times 10^{-323}$, barely two steps above
          the smallest double. Writing \texttt{a*b**x} therefore loses
          $15\%$ before $a$ is applied. Python is not at fault: it returns
          the correctly rounded value of what it was asked. The ordering
          avoids asking.
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Rule used.} While the running product is above 1, multiply by the
  smallest factor left; otherwise multiply by the largest. Opposing factors
  cancel as they are applied, so the running product stays near 1.
  \smallskip

  \textbf{Scope (DC-04, NFR-05).} This covers every input for which
  $|b|^{n/2}$ is representable. Beyond that the exact result may still be
  representable, so reporting an overflow would be untrue: the calculator
  raises \texttt{AlgorithmRangeError} instead. Never a wrong value, and
  never a wrong reason. About 1 input in 20{,}000 across 600 decades, none
  inside the persona's working range.
\end{frame}
